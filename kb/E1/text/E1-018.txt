ARTICLE
Study on textile waste generation in the undifferentiated municipal solid waste stream in Guimarães, Portugal
Alona Perebynos1 · Dalila Sepúlveda2 · Carlos Ribeiro1
Received: 26 September 2022 / Revised: 12 January 2023 / Accepted: 31 January 2023 © Zhejiang University Press 2023
Abstract
Guimarães is a middle sized city and municipality located in northern Portugal. The municipality has committed to reducing the annual amount of undifferentiated municipal solid waste (MSW) from 371 kg/capita in 2021 to 120 kg/capita by 2030 under the Zero Waste Cities Certification process. In the municipality of Guimarães, one of the constant fractions of MSW composition is textile waste (TW), which the revised EU Waste Framework Directive requires separate collection by 1 January 2025. Therefore, two indicators of TW generation were analysed to identify waste collection routes with a high level of textile waste generation for the priority implementation of separate collection: TW composition in the undifferentiated MSW stream and TW generation per capita. Basic statistical analysis methods were used to process the source data of TW composition in the undifferentiated MSW stream. Cluster analysis was applied to the data set on TW generation per capita, considering the area typology (urban, rural or mixed) of collection routes. It was considered that 39% of the industrial sector of Guimarães consists of textile and clothing production and represents small- and medium-sized enterprises, which can affect TW generation in the undifferentiated MSW stream. Causal-comparative research was used to define the correlation between TW generation per capita and the economic activity of the textile and clothing industry in the municipality. As a result, applying a multi-disciplinary approach, a project of the Textile Waste Generation Map was presented.

Introduction
Textiles have long become an integral part of our everyday life and society, providing us with clothing, shoes and carpets, curtains, etc. for homes, offices, and other public buildings. Worldwide, the textile and clothing (T&C) industry employs millions of people and creates enormous economic value. It is considered one of the largest sectors in Europe, with 143,000 companies with a turnover of 147 billion Euros and 1.3 million employees in 2021 [1, 2]. As stated by EURATEX, Portugal is among the top 5 employers, with a turnover of 5% in the textile and fashion industry among all regions of the European Union (EU) (Italy—22%, Romania—11%, Poland—11%, Portugal—9%, Germany—9%) [1]. According to data collected by the Associação Têxtil e Vestuário de Portugal in 2019, this industrial segment employs more than 139 thousand workers, with more than 5000 companies and a turnover of more than 7700 million euros. The T&C production companies are mainly located in northern of Portugal (Porto, Braga, Guimarães, Famalicão) [3, 4]. The industrial sector of Guimarães consists of 25% textile production and 14% clothing production. It accounts for approximately 5% of textile production and 7% of clothing production in Portugal.
However, the significant ecological footprint of the T&C industry, as well as the health and social risks during production processes, have become the focus of increasing attention. The production and consumption of textiles and clothing cause a significant environmental impact by using resources, water, land, and chemicals and emitting greenhouse gases and pollutants. Joint Research Centre (JRC) Technical report states that between 1.7 million tonnes and 2.1 million tonnes of post-consumer textiles are separately collected annually throughout the EU for recycling or sale on global reuse markets, representing approximately 38% of new textiles placed on the EU market. Consequently, the remaining 62% is discarded in the undifferentiated municipal solid waste (MSW) stream [9]. In the EU Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles, it is declared that there is significant potential to reduce TW and ensure that it creates further value by boosting its preparation for reuse and recycling [10].
The revised [11] set specific deadlines for achieving recycling targets. Starting in 2025, at least 55% of municipal waste should be recycled including used textiles. A recycling rate of at least 60% is set for 2030 and at least 65% for 2035. Recycling involves recovery methods such as preparation for reuse and material recycling. Thermal recovery and landfill are not included in the recycling rate.
According to the EU Directive, the landfill rate for municipal waste may not exceed 10% from 2035 [11]. While in the past the motivation for the separate collection of used textiles was mostly socially or economically driven, the revised Waste Framework Directive of the EU set a requirement to implement the separate collection of TW by 1 January 2025 in the municipal waste management system.
The main objective of this study is to determine MSW routes with a high content of TW by analysing waste generation indicators for the priority implementation of separate collections of TW. As a result, the “Textile Waste Generation Map” project is presented by applying a multi-disciplinary approach that includes methods of statistical analysis and causal research and takes into account variables such as socio-demographic characteristics, territorial typologies and economic activity of the administrative units of Guimarães.
Theoretical background
Several trends in the T&C industry seem to be moving in the right direction. Consumers are increasingly conscious about a brand’s purpose, as well as the social and environmental impacts of their purchases [12, 13]. A survey based on interviews with more than 27,000 people in the EU Member States showed that 80% of respondents expressed a desire for clothing lasting longer and 77% of Europeans agree that clothing should only be made of materials that can be recycled [1]. According to the results of the research [14] on the behaviour of consumers regarding the disposal of textile products, more targeted information campaigns should be implemented to support proper handling options with post-consumer textiles, ideally in collaboration with policymakers at both national and municipal levels. The focus should be on consumers who do not separate or have a lower propensity to separate unusable or worn textiles—men in general, less educated consumers, and those with lower income [14].
The Platform for Accelerating the Circular Economy (PACE) has identified three circular objectives for the T&C industry in its report “The Circular Economy Action Agenda for Textiles” [2] based on a global circular economy vision [15, 16]:
1. Material inputs for textiles are safe for both workers and consumers, renewable (e.g., cotton or wood-based fibre obtained using sustainable agricultural practices), recycled (synthetic or natural fibres), or both (e.g. recycled plant-based fibres).
2. Textiles are kept in use: this objective concerns both production (all products that are made are used, and excess stock is minimised and never destroyed) and consumption (all products are used repeatedly and for long periods of time, instead of being left unused or thrown away prematurely).
3. Textiles are recyclable and recycled at the end of life (upcycling should be prioritised over downcycling).
In general, TW can be divided into post-industrial waste coming from production sites and post-consumer textile waste (PCTW). While industrial TW often has good quality and large volumes of homogeneous materials, post-consumer waste is often heterogeneous and consists of large volumes of mixed textiles [17]. In Europe, on average, MSW is composed around 4% to 5% by weight of textile [14]. In accordance with the circular economy concept [10, 15], used textiles and clothing should be handled with proper waste management and through a defined waste hierarchy. The waste hierarchy is applied in the Waste Framework Directive of the EU as a priority order in the waste management system

(reduce–reuse–recycle–recovery–disposal) [11]. Stages of the textile life cycle, such as raw material production, manufacturing, and use, affect the environment the most, but circular economy practices (such as a separate collection of TW at the source followed by reuse or recycling) can mitigate the related impact [18]. A study by Amicarelli and Bux on quantifying textile streams and recycling prospects in Europe [19] states that “separate collection should be considered as one of the most important pathways to: (a) reduce TW streams destined to incineration or landfilling; (b) reduce virgin fibres supplying from the environment; (c) reduce CO2 eq emissions associated with the end-of-life pathways, such as incineration or landfilling”.
Each country of the EU has a different level of success in collecting PCTW. Common ways to collect PCTW are MSW collection, collection containers, kerbside collection, collection through mail back options, retailer and charity shop drop-offs, and home pick-ups [20]. Larger PCTW becomes part of municipal waste and is collected in bins for undifferentiated MSW. This makes it completely unsuitable for reuse or recycling according to the waste hierarchy due to its contamination. Some quantities of PCTW are collected and handled centrally in recycling centres operated, for example, by municipalities or collected via publicly accessible containers of private companies or charitable organisations. Part of the used textiles is sold to sorting companies for further processing. The remaining part ends up in incineration plants or landfills [17].
Although the separate collection of PCTW will become obligatory, efficient reuse and recycling cannot be achieved by simply collecting textiles separately from municipal waste. In order to properly reuse or recycle PCTW, it is necessary to have information on the characteristics (e.g., material composition, quantity, removal methods) of used textiles to ensure the best environmentally sound way of handling it [21]. The sorting of collected textiles determines which path the item goes through. The more accurately the sorting is adapted, the more pieces of clothing can be used for reuse as second-hand goods or high-quality recycling [22]. According to a survey in Germany, sorters sort textiles into an average of 154 different fractions, with a maximum of 400 varieties [17].
Treatment options for clothing and household textiles largely depend on whether the items have been separated at the source, that is, separated from other waste at the point of generation. Suppose there was no separate collection, and the items are mixed with non-textiles and contaminated. In that case, there is the option to landfill or incinerate [17].
However, TW, which was collected separately, sorted, and identified as end-of-life textile products, enters the production cycle through recycling processes (Fig. 1). The most common way of recycling PCTW is classical mechanical technologies that break down the fabric and retain the fibres by cutting, tearing, shredding, or carding. These recovered fibres are almost exclusively used in nonwoven products for insulation, upholstery, and automotive textiles [17, 23].
Physical methods use physical processes to make the fibres or polymers suitable for reprocessing, either by melting or dissolving them, after which either melt spinning or solution spinning can be used to form a new filament (i.e., a fibre of infinite length) [23]. Chemical recycling of textiles is still largely under development and is applied to dissolve synthetic or natural fibres by chemical compounds so they can be used as recovered raw materials [2, 23].
In the circular fashion chain, raw materials should preferably come from renewable resources, recycled materials or TW, PCTW or residues produced in the factories in the form of yarns or fabrics (post-industrial) [24]. As mentioned previously, there are distinct differences between post-industrial and post-consumer textile streams. Post-industrial waste streams are often well defined and more homogeneous, as they can be collected as fibres, yarns, or fabrics, thus well before the garment is produced. Thus, TW from industrial sources has a much better chance of achieving cost-effective, high-quality recycling than PCTW [23, 25].
In contrast to the post-industrial waste stream, the PCTW stream is more complex, poorly defined, and contaminated and has decreased fibre quality due to washing and wearing. In addition, each fibre has its optimal recycling option (Fig. 1). For instance, in order to improve the functionality of a fabric, manufacturers combine two or more fibre types to form a new yarn or fabric that can combine the best qualities of each fibre. A well-known example is the presence of a few percent elastane in jeans to improve wearing comfort.

Currently, the non-reusable fraction of collected textiles is mostly downcycled into industrial rags, upholstery filling, and insulation, or incinerated or landfilled. Less than 1% of TW is recycled into new fibres for clothing as technologies for processing textiles to recycled fibres are only starting to emerge [20, 25]. Increasing the recycling rate requires changes throughout the entire life cycle of textiles: textiles must be designed to be easily disassembled and economically recycled, consumers must dispose of them properly, and collection/sorting systems must effectively separate different TW streams to avoid cross-contamination [2].
In Portugal, the Strategic Plan for Urban Waste states that there are already successful national experiences in the TW collection for reuse or recycling either through the municipalities or Urban Waste Management Systems in partnership with social solidarity institutions or through clothing sales stores, which encourage the delivery of used clothing so that it can be changed into a new garment [27]. The municipality of Guimarães has been implementing a circular economy policy through the program Guimarães for Circular Economy (G4CE) [28], which in 2022 was rebranded with the name of RRRCICLO—Circular Economy in Guimarães and integrates a strong awareness campaign for the community. The strategy is one of the examples of the Governance Ecosystem—Guimarães 2030, implemented in 2014, which brings together the public and private sectors, universities, and citizens [29]. The municipality of Guimarães, as a local owner entity responsible for providing municipal waste management services, recognises its role in creating a closed loop in the circular T&C sector by providing residents with a separate collection of TW.
Materials and methods
Guimarães is a middle size city and municipality located in northern Portugal, which historic centre is UNESCO World Heritage. According to the 2021 Census, Guimarães has 156,849 inhabitants distributed over 241 km2 [30]. In terms of administrative organisation, the territory has 48 parishes (administrative units), with a group of 17 medium size agglomerations, and 31 of those can be considered as small size. Guimarães has 35 different collection routes for undifferentiated MSW covering all 48 parishes. 31 routes with the door-to-door system are managed by the municipality and 4 routes with underground waste containers with a capacity between 3000 L and 5000 L, responsibility for the operation of which belongs to a public company “Vitrus Ambiente EM SA”.
Since this study aims to identify the waste collection routes with a high content of TW for the priority implementation of its separate collection, two indicators of waste generation were analysed: TW composition in the undifferentiated MSW stream and TW generation per capita.
TW generation
The source data for this research were obtained by Centre for Waste Valorisation, a non-profit institution that offers research, scientific analysis, and actual application services in the waste valorisation area, during the survey of the physical composition of undifferentiated MSW in 2016. The results of the survey were published in the article “Municipal Waste Map: a Case Study of Guimarães, Portugal” [31]. The characterisation of undifferentiated MSW was performed considering the Portuguese law requirements Portaria No. 851/2009, 7th August, 2009 [32] that states: (1) the minimum number of samples for characterisation of the physical composition of undifferentiated MSW is 21; and (2) the quantity of material per sample is 350 kg. Accordingly, 26 routes with door-to-door waste collection and 3 routes with collection by underground containers were analysed, which in total corresponds to 29 samples weighing 350 kg each. All door-to-door collection routes were sorted and numbered according to the period and frequency of waste collection. A letter in the name of the route indicates the frequency of waste collection: “A”—Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, “B”—Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday, and routes without a letter—daily from Monday to Saturday. For the survey of the physical composition of undifferentiated MSW, 26 out of 31 routes with door-to-door waste collection were selected because 5 of them are similar in terms of population and area typology, so they were considered together: 7A with 7B, 10A with 10B, 12A with 12B, 15A with 15B and 16A with 16B. The characterisation of undifferentiated MSW was undertaken twice in April and June for the autumn–winter and spring–summer periods, respectively [31]. The sampling campaign of undifferentiated MSW covered 92% of the municipality’s area with 94% of the population.
Basic statistical analysis methods were used to process the source data of the TW composition in the undifferentiated MSW stream. The z-score, standard deviation, median, and mean were calculated. The z-score (standard score) is a numerical measurement that describes a value’s relationship to the arithmetic mean of a sample. Basically, the z-score value shows how many standard deviations separate the value from the arithmetic mean. A standard deviation is a measure of how dispersed the data are in relation to the arithmetic mean. A low standard deviation (<1) indicates that the data are clustered around the arithmetic mean, while a high standard deviation (>1) implies that the data are more spread out [33, 34].

Results and discussion
TW generation
From the obtained data of the survey of the physical composition of undifferentiated MSW [31], the composition of TW (%) was excluded for the characterisation of TW generation in the municipality (Table 1). Only 26 routes with door-todoor waste collection were considered for this research, and routes with underground containers were excluded because they are placed in high-density urban areas throughout the municipality, which makes it impossible to calculate the exact residence population required for further analysis. To process the source data of the TW composition in undifferentiated MSW, the average value for each route was calculated by taking the mean of the autumn–winter and spring–summer periods. Route 17 had no data on the TW generation, so it was subsequently excluded from the calculations, and the number of routes was reduced to 25.
The next step in data processing was the calculation of a “z-score” for the sample of average values of the TW composition in undifferentiated MSW. The standard deviation and arithmetic mean of the sample of average values of TW composition are 3.4 and 6.1, respectively. In this case, corresponding to the z-score >1 condition, routes 3B and 4B were excluded from the sample as outliers. After excluding routes 3B and 4B, 23 routes remained in the sample of average values of the TW composition in undifferentiated MSW (Fig. 2). In addition, for the sample of 23 routes, the values of the median and the arithmetic mean were calculated, which are equal to 4.5 and 5.4, respectively. The median value was more suitable to identify the excessive TW generation values in the obtained sample of 23 routes, because the sample still had skewed distributions with a standard deviation equal to 2.1. The median value of 4.5% coincides with the average value of 4%–5% TW composition in the MSW in European countries [8, 14]. After processing the source data, the number of waste collection routes in TW generation research decreased from 26 to 23, covering 81% of the municipality’s area with 84% of the population. Using the median value of the indicator of TW composition in the undifferentiated MSW stream, the following routes with a high content of TW were chosen: 2A, 2B, 3A, 5A, 6A, 8A, 8B, 10A, 11B, 14A, 15B, and 16B.

The evaluation of the economic activity of the municipality showed that the routes from the urban and mixed clusters are characterised by a significantly larger number of enterprises in both T&C manufacture and T&C trade than the routes from the rural cluster. It should also be noted that among the routes of all clusters, there are more T&C manufacture enterprises than trade companies. However, only the number of manufacturing enterprises was used for the causal-comparative research, as trade companies tend to sell textile products rather than generate the consequential amount of TW and mix it with the undifferentiated MSW stream.
In this study, we hypothesised two scenarios for the causal-comparative research of two variables: TW generation per capita and the economic activity of the T&C industry. The first scenario assumes the existence of small-scale clothing production in local households, that is, an unregistered economic activity. Such private economic activity is usually accompanied by the mixing of TW with municipal waste. In order to carry out the selection more accurately, the routes were divided into subclusters based on the population in each area typology cluster (Table 4).
The subcluster formed in the urban cluster were 12A+13A, 1A+1B, and 15B, and 13B, 14B, 11B, and 5A; in the rural cluster—10A+14A, 11A, and 4A, and 8A, 3A, 5B, 8B, and 6A; and in the urban cluster—16B, 7B and 6B, 2B, and 2A.
The following indicator of causality was used to identify the routes according to the first scenario: a high level of TW generation per capita with insignificant economic activity compared to others in the subcluster. According to the indicator of causality of the first scenario, 1A+1B, 11B, 14B, 15B (mixed area typology cluster), 8A, 8B (rural area typology cluster), and 2A (urban area typology cluster) routes were identified.

Discussion
The main task of this research was to identify waste collection routes with a high content of TW in the undifferentiated MSW stream. To fulfil this task, the study was conducted using the following indicators: TW composition (%) in undifferentiated MSW; monthly TW generation per capita (kg/capita) and the correlation of TW generation per capita to the economic activity of the T&C industry, which was represented as the number of textile or clothing manufacturers per route. Table 6 shows the summary results after processing all indicators of TW generation. The mixed area typology cluster holds 4 waste collection routes and 1 unit of merged routes that have been classified with at least one positive indicator of TW generation which means a high content of TW. In addition, 4 of them (1A+1B, 5A, 11B, and 15B) were engaged in creating the Textile Waste Generation Map (Fig. 4). The rural area typology cluster includes 4 waste collection routes and 1 unit of merged routes that have been classified with at least one positive indicator of TW generation. Conclusions
Based on the data collected and analysed, it is expected that the investigation will become relevant in the development of the separate collection of TW. Capping the content of TW in the undifferentiated MSW stream is necessary to divert it for further recycling. This is possible by optimising the existing routes or developing new TW collection routes, as well as by installing new collection points for reusable textiles. All over Europe, as well as in the municipality of Guimarães, there is a system of containers for old “fit for use” clothes provided by various charities.
However, a huge amount of other old or unneeded textiles go to the containers and become a part of MSW, which is usually incinerated or landfilled without any sorting or the possibility of recycling [17].
In the study of developing the Municipal Waste Map of Guimarães [31], it was specified that TW represents an average of 5.32% of the total undifferentiated waste composition. It is also noted that the highest percentage of TW was found on routes with large containers, which is a clue that small and medium enterprises do not have a private collection for this kind of waste and put it where they find containers. In addition, 1/3 of the Guimarães population is located in the urban area, where most of the services and equipment are implemented, while 2/3 of the population is placed outside the central agglomeration, the area registered for both industrial activity and agricultural practices. A possible solution to the question of separate collection of TW in the municipality of Guimarães can be the implementation of special marked bags in the pay-asyou-throw (PAYT) system. PAYT system already exists in the city centre of Guimarães, and by 2028, it is planned to be extended to the entire territory of the municipality [41]. Colour-coded bags for end-of-life textiles will help to separate TW from the undifferentiated MSW stream at the source and should be offered to both households and businesses.
This study proposes the project of the Textile Waste Generation Map with marked administrative units of the municipality with a high level of TW generation where the separate collection of TW should be implemented first.
